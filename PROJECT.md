# War of Resonance

*(Formerly titled "Aether Engine Tactics.")*

War of Resonance is a compact, turn-based strategy RPG that draws tactical inspiration from the lane-based battles of *Heavenstrike Rivals* and narrative inspiration from *Final Fantasy Tactics: War of the Lions*, *Tactics Ogre*, and *Triangle Strategy*. Players assemble a squad of units, deploy them onto a small battlefield, and use their class behaviors and abilities to defeat the enemy Conductor.

This is a spiritual successor, not a remake. Its setting, characters, artwork, audio, story, terminology, interface, and progression will be original.

## Narrative Foundation

Everything in this section is established canon unless a subsection is explicitly marked as a hidden in-world truth, an unresolved mystery, or a future design decision. Gameplay systems described elsewhere in this document remain in force where they do not conflict with this canon; contradictions are flagged in place rather than silently rewritten.

### Project Vision

The story emphasizes political intrigue, factional conflict, unreliable history, betrayal, assassination, competing ideologies, and morally compromised leaders. The central conflict is not simply about energy. It is about power, control, legitimacy, history, and who has the right to decide civilization's future.

- Avoid a simple good-versus-evil structure. Every faction should have legitimate strengths, serious flaws, internal divisions, and understandable reasons for its actions.
- Victories should often carry consequences. Winning a battle may worsen a political crisis, create a monopoly, trigger retaliation, destabilize infrastructure, or empower a more dangerous actor.
- The player should frequently succeed tactically while being outmaneuvered politically.
- Important characters may survive battles only to be removed through assassination, betrayal, economic pressure, propaganda, legal action, diplomatic compromise, or decisions made by their own faction.
- The final story should not end with every faction joining hands in uncomplicated unity. Cooperation may occur, but it should remain fragile, conditional, politically costly, and vulnerable to manipulation.

### Setting: The Caelian Empire

Centuries ago, the known world was unified under the Caelian Empire. Its capital was Caelis.

The Caelian Empire represented the height of technological civilization. Its prosperity, military strength, transportation, communications, industry, and public infrastructure were all built around an ancient energy system known as the Source.

The Source was located within or beneath Caelis. It supplied energy throughout the Empire through a vast network of buried conduits, relay stations, autonomous machines, and infrastructure nodes.

To modern civilizations, surviving Caelian technology appears impossibly advanced. It does not clearly resemble any one modern energy discipline, and most scholars assume that the Source operated through principles that have since been lost.

The Source was more than a generator. It was the central system upon which Caelian civilization depended. Control of the Source was inseparable from control of the Empire.

Then the Caelian Empire disappeared. No surviving public account provides a complete explanation.

### The Public History of Caelis

Modern people generally believe that the Caelian Empire collapsed because of some combination of technological overreach, civil conflict, resource exhaustion, political decay, or catastrophe involving the Source.

Each faction teaches a different version of the Empire's fall:

- Coal histories claim the Caelians exhausted the world and concealed the true cost of their prosperity.
- Steam histories claim the Empire became complacent, centralized, and incapable of maintaining the systems it had built.
- Solar histories claim the Caelians lost balance in their pursuit of limitless power.
- Wind traditions claim the Empire collapsed because it attempted to control systems that should have remained distributed and free.
- Fusion scholars claim the Empire reached the edge of a technological breakthrough but lacked the courage or stability to complete it.

Every version contains fragments of truth. None contains the complete truth.

### The Great Lie

> **Hidden in-world truth.** This is what actually happened, but no present-day faction knows it. Do not surface it in early exposition.

The Caelian Empire did not collapse in the way modern history describes. Its leaders intentionally dismantled it.

They concluded that no government, dynasty, military order, or individual should possess complete control of the Source. Rather than allow the Source to remain under one centralized power, the Empire was deliberately fractured into successor states.

Records were destroyed or altered. Knowledge was divided. Conductor technology was restricted. Maps were redrawn. Institutions were separated. Later generations inherited fragments of the original system without understanding the whole.

Modern civilization exists within the aftermath of a deliberate historical cover-up. No present-day faction possesses the complete truth. Some institutions may preserve pieces of it, but even those groups should have incomplete, biased, or self-serving interpretations.

### The Source

Publicly, the Source is understood as a lost Caelian energy system capable of producing or distributing enormous amounts of power. Its exact nature is unknown.

Different factions believe the Source validates their own technology or offers the means to make their energy discipline dominant:

- Coal may view it as access to an inexhaustible reserve.
- Steam may view it as the ultimate engine.
- Solar may view it as a perfect clean-energy system.
- Wind may view it as proof that energy can be distributed without centralized ownership.
- Fusion may view it as the final step toward effectively limitless output.

These interpretations should be politically useful but technically incomplete.

The Source should remain mysterious for much of the story. Early exposition should focus on its scale, importance, and lost function rather than explaining how it works.

### Hidden Historical Truth

> **Hidden in-world truth.** Do not present this as common knowledge or early exposition. It should emerge gradually through evidence rather than a single exposition scene.

The Caelian Empire did not rely on a single mysterious energy process in the way modern historians assume. The Source was an integrated system capable of receiving, converting, balancing, storing, and distributing energy produced through many different processes.

The modern energy factions inherited incomplete fragments of that larger system. Coal, Steam, Solar, Wind, and Fusion are not unrelated technologies that independently replaced Caelian science. They are incomplete descendants of a broader Caelian architecture whose original purpose has been forgotten. The modern factions mistake their fragment for the whole.

Possible evidence includes:

- Caelian ruins containing systems claimed by more than one faction.
- Ancient machines responding to multiple energy technologies.
- Modern Conductors discovering that their supposedly incompatible augmentations share a common foundational design.
- Faction archives containing matching diagrams with different labels.
- Relays that require several energy processes to reactivate.
- Robots carrying fragmented memories of pre-fall Caelian systems.
- Structures beneath Caelis that reveal the modern divisions were imposed after the Empire dissolved.

The eventual realization should be: the factions were never developing separate paths toward the future. They were each preserving one broken piece of the past.

### The Modern Factions

The modern world is divided into major political powers built around different energy technologies and the social systems that grew around them. These are not merely visual themes or combat classes. Each faction should function as a government, economic system, military power, cultural identity, and political ideology.

#### Coal

Coal represents extraction, labor, heavy industry, logistics, mining, fuel, and material production. Its society believes civilization is built by those willing to perform difficult, dangerous, and unglamorous work. Coal provides raw materials and fuel upon which other systems often depend.

- Strengths: endurance, production, fortification, resource control, practical engineering.
- Flaws: exploitation, environmental destruction, entrenched industrial interests, class hierarchy, labor abuse, resistance to technological change.

Coal should not be treated as inherently evil or primitive. Without Coal infrastructure, large parts of civilization may fail.

#### Steam

Steam represents pressure, mechanical engineering, disciplined systems, transportation, centralized industry, and durable machinery. Its culture values structure, reliability, planning, tradition, and precision. Steam built much of the modern world's transport and manufacturing infrastructure.

- Strengths: durability, armor, controlled output, logistics, dependable engineering.
- Flaws: bureaucracy, rigid hierarchy, centralization, political conservatism, protection of obsolete institutions.

#### Solar

Solar represents light, efficiency, optimization, advanced materials, precision, clean energy, and ideological confidence. Its society often views itself as the enlightened future. Solar cities may be technologically elegant, highly ordered, and publicly committed to progress.

- Strengths: shielding, ranged power, stored charge, efficiency, high-output precision systems.
- Flaws: elitism, moral arrogance, religious or ideological purity, exclusion, dependence on controlled environments, and the belief that technological cleanliness implies political virtue.

#### Wind

Wind represents mobility, decentralization, autonomy, distributed infrastructure, rapid movement, and resistance to centralized control. Its communities may exist across mobile settlements, high-altitude platforms, open trade routes, or loosely connected regional alliances.

- Strengths: speed, repositioning, evasion, disruption, flexible logistics, distributed systems.
- Flaws: political fragmentation, weak central authority, piracy, inconsistent law, internal rivalry, difficulty sustaining large institutions.

#### Fusion

Fusion represents scientific ambition, high-risk innovation, concentrated power, experimental technology, and the belief that progress requires danger. Its society pushes beyond accepted technical and ethical limits.

- Strengths: enormous output, advanced weapons, overcharging, unstable burst power, elite systems.
- Flaws: secrecy, human experimentation, catastrophic failure, institutional arrogance, willingness to sacrifice lives for breakthroughs.

No faction is purely correct. No faction is purely corrupt. Each should contain internal political blocs, reformers, traditionalists, profiteers, idealists, workers, military leaders, and ordinary citizens with conflicting interests.

### Conductors

Conductors are permanently augmented individuals capable of synchronizing with autonomous machine forces and major infrastructure systems. They are not ordinary officers. They serve as military leaders, political symbols, engineers, strategic coordinators, and living interfaces between people, robots, and energy systems.

Conductors are typically bonded to one energy discipline. Their bodies incorporate cybernetic elements associated with the faction they serve:

- A Coal Conductor may use industrial lungs, reinforced limbs, mineral sensors, furnace systems, and extraction equipment.
- A Steam Conductor may use pressure regulators, valves, brass or steel prosthetics, boiler systems, and mechanical control interfaces.
- A Solar Conductor may use photovoltaic skin, lens-like eyes, radiant implants, reflective armor, and stored-light systems.
- A Wind Conductor may use lightweight skeletal reinforcement, turbine organs, airflow sensors, stabilizers, and high-mobility augmentations.
- A Fusion Conductor may use containment systems, magnetic implants, plasma channels, reactor-like organs, and radiation shielding.

Conductors do not simply command robots through rank. They synchronize with them. The relationship should feel closer to resonance, coordination, or shared systems than simple ownership.

Robots can function without a Conductor, but they lose higher-order coordination, formation abilities, advanced synchronization, and access to certain strategic systems.

Conductors should be treated as national assets. The loss of a Conductor may cause military collapse, infrastructure failure, factory shutdowns, trade disruption, power shortages, or political crisis. Assassinating a Conductor may be equivalent to attacking an entire region. Because of this, Conductors are often protected, controlled, celebrated, feared, or used as tools by their own governments.

### The Player Character

The player is the first publicly known Conductor capable of synchronizing with multiple energy disciplines. This should not be explained as prophecy, destiny, divine selection, or simple superiority. The player is an anomaly created by the opening relay disaster: an expedition technician critically injured during a concealed relay-activation experiment and kept alive by emergency cybernetics that were never designed to operate together, which the active relay bound into a functional multi-discipline system. Their compatibility was not the intended result of the experiment, and their survival was not expected. See Player Origin and Opening Tutorial.

NPC Conductors are highly specialized. They may achieve extraordinary synchronization with one faction but cannot safely interface with others. The player has broader compatibility. This explains why the player can build mixed-faction teams while most NPC forces remain specialized.

The player should not begin as the strongest Conductor. Their distinguishing trait is adaptability. The player may initially synchronize less efficiently than veteran faction Conductors but possesses the potential to connect systems others cannot.

Different factions should react to the player differently. Some want to recruit them. Some want to study them. Some want to control them. Some want to copy the process. Some believe their existence threatens the political order. Some want them eliminated before cross-faction synchronization becomes reproducible.

The player's motivations should develop through the story. They may initially seek survival, answers about their augmentation, protection for allies, or the truth about Caelis. Over time, they become entangled in conflicts much larger than their original goals.

The player should not automatically be morally superior to NPC Conductors. They should face their own compromises, failures, blind spots, and consequences.

### Player Origin and Opening Tutorial

The player begins as a technician assigned to a joint reclamation expedition investigating a dormant Caelian relay.

The expedition is publicly presented as routine salvage and infrastructure recovery. Its stated purpose is to reclaim usable Caelian machines, components, records, and relay technology from a previously inaccessible site. This establishes why the expedition already fields a small roster of machine units: they have been recovered from the site, restored during earlier reclamation work, or assigned to protect the salvage team.

The reclamation operation is the foundation of the game's tutorial. The opening missions gradually teach:

- Selecting and deploying recovered units.
- Unit classes, movement, and attack range.
- Basic machine synchronization.
- Reclaiming or activating dormant units.
- Interacting with Caelian machinery.
- Protecting technicians and salvage equipment.
- Completing objectives other than destroying every enemy.
- The distinction between an ordinary technician directing machines and a true Conductor synchronizing with them.

At the beginning, the player is not yet recognized as a Conductor. They operate the recovered units through standard control systems and expedition protocols. Cassian serves as the expedition's logistics adjutant and helps coordinate personnel, recovered machines, supplies, and extraction routes.

#### The Relay Disaster

During the reclamation operation, the expedition reaches the dormant relay's central activation chamber. The activation is presented to most members of the expedition as a routine attempt to restore the relay and access additional salvage zones.

> **Hidden in-world truth.** The expedition's leadership has concealed the true purpose of the operation. The relay activation is an intentional experiment involving recovered Caelian interface technology — a controlled test of whether the relay could be restored and whether modern interfaces could synchronize with it. Most expedition personnel, including the player and Cassian, do not know they are participating in a test. The player was not knowingly selected as a test subject, and the people who authorized the experiment did not expect the relay to create a multi-compatible Conductor.

During activation, the relay unexpectedly synchronizes with the combined architecture present at the site: the player's technical interface, the recovered Caelian systems, and machine components originating from multiple modern factions. The resulting synchronization destabilizes the facility. Machines activate without authorization. Previously dormant units begin responding to the player. The expedition is attacked, trapped, or divided as the relay attempts to establish a wider connection.

The player is critically injured during the disaster. Emergency cybernetic components and interface systems are used to keep them alive; these components are not designed to operate together, but the active relay binds them into a functional system. The player becomes the first publicly known multi-compatible Conductor.

The public explanation is that the relay disaster was an accidental activation caused by unstable Caelian machinery. The official report conceals the experimental purpose of the expedition.

The player's immediate motivations after the disaster:

- Survive the effects of the transformation.
- Rescue remaining expedition members.
- Recover the machine units that now respond to them.
- Understand why the relay synchronized with them.
- Determine who authorized the experiment.
- Discover why the official account of the disaster is false.
- Protect Cassian and the other survivors from being blamed, detained, or silenced.

The tutorial ends with the player escaping the relay site with Cassian and a small mixed roster of reclaimed machine units. This creates the initial player force naturally through the story rather than granting the player an unexplained army.

### NPC Conductors

NPC Conductors should not be simple faction mascots. They should be major political actors with distinct motivations, loyalties, ambitions, and limits.

Some may be loyal to their government. Some may serve their people while opposing their leaders. Some may be reformers. Some may be opportunists. Some may believe authoritarian control is necessary to prevent wider catastrophe. Some may genuinely want peace but accept actions the player considers unforgivable.

Conductors should be capable of being outmaneuvered and removed without losing in battle. Possible outcomes include:

- Assassination during peace negotiations.
- Poisoning after a successful mission.
- Political arrest.
- Removal from office through fabricated charges.
- Infrastructure access being revoked by their own government.
- Forced retirement.
- Public execution.
- Defection followed by historical erasure.
- Capture and reprogramming.
- Loss of public support through propaganda.
- Economic isolation that makes their military command impossible.
- Being sacrificed by their faction to preserve an alliance.

A Conductor may be undefeated on the battlefield and still lose everything.

### Narrative Tone

The narrative should draw from the political and tragic structure of *Final Fantasy Tactics: War of the Lions*. The player should repeatedly discover that battlefield strength is not the same as political power.

Good people may make terrible decisions. Self-interested people may create stability. Idealists may cause disaster. Pragmatists may prevent catastrophe at an unacceptable moral cost. Villains should often have understandable goals. Heroes should often be compromised.

Avoid resolving every conflict through friendship, mutual understanding, or a final alliance against a clearly evil enemy. Temporary cooperation is acceptable, but it should be shaped by leverage, necessity, fear, debt, common enemies, or political calculation.

### The Player's Political Rival: Cassian

The player's primary political rival is Cassian (no surname assigned yet). Cassian occupies a role similar in narrative function to Delita in *Final Fantasy Tactics*, without copying that character directly.

Cassian begins as the player's closest surviving companion from the opening relay disaster, serving as the expedition's logistics adjutant responsible for:

- Expedition supplies, machine-unit inventories, and reclamation records.
- Personnel assignments and transport/extraction planning.
- Communications between participating factions.
- Reporting recovered Caelian assets and coordinating access to restricted salvage areas.

Cassian is not originally a Conductor and begins politically insignificant: no major military rank, no powerful family title, no important institution. Cassian survives the relay disaster because the player saves him, establishing a genuine personal bond and a lasting sense of obligation between the two.

Cassian should not begin as deceptive, manipulative, or secretly ambitious. At the start of the story, Cassian is practical, resourceful, loyal to the player, and focused on keeping the expedition survivors alive.

#### Parallel Rise

Following the disaster, the player becomes a politically dangerous multi-compatible Conductor but lacks the knowledge and influence required to survive the resulting attention. Cassian becomes the person who helps the player navigate those consequences, initially handling:

- Official investigations and conflicting faction claims over the player.
- Custody disputes concerning reclaimed machine units.
- Negotiations for medical treatment and legal protection.
- Public explanations for the relay disaster, and suppression or correction of false reports.
- Access to restricted territories, and communication with officials the player cannot reach directly.
- Compensation and protection for survivors.

The player gains power through battlefield victories. Cassian gains power through political leverage. Cassian's methods include managing public perception, negotiating access between factions, cultivating officials, controlling reports, trading favors, deciding which truths become public, building relationships with administrators, merchants, officers, and civil leaders, presenting the player's victories in politically useful ways, protecting selected people while allowing others to take blame, and turning temporary agreements into permanent influence.

Cassian's development is gradual. Early in the story, Cassian uses influence primarily to protect the player and the survivors. Later, Cassian begins withholding information because he believes disclosure would endanger the player or destabilize negotiations. Eventually, Cassian starts deciding which truths society is capable of surviving.

Cassian's political rise occurs in parallel with the player's military rise. As the player recruits Conductors and wins battles, Cassian gains access to the leaders, institutions, and negotiations surrounding those victories, and frequently turns the player's tactical accomplishments into political concessions:

- Converting a rescued city into a voting bloc.
- Using recovered Caelian records to pressure an official.
- Trading access to the player's machine forces for legal recognition.
- Negotiating ceasefires that strengthen Cassian's coalition.
- Controlling how a dead Conductor is remembered.
- Reframing an apparent defeat as justification for institutional reform.
- Sealing evidence in exchange for the release of prisoners.
- Granting factions limited access to recovered technology in return for political support.

Cassian sometimes protects the player by controlling or suppressing information without asking permission. This should initially seem necessary and beneficial; over time, it becomes clear that Cassian is constructing an official version of events.

#### The Coalition

By the final act, Cassian has created a political coalition stronger than any single faction. It is not a simple alliance of idealists; it includes factions and institutions with conflicting interests that support Cassian for different reasons:

- Moderate faction leaders seeking an end to the war.
- Industrial interests seeking predictable access to energy.
- Regional governments afraid of faction domination.
- Conductors who want legal protection.
- Officials who fear public exposure of Caelian history.
- Civilian organizations desperate to stabilize infrastructure.
- Machine representatives or custodial systems seeking lawful recognition.
- Opportunists who expect positions within the new order.

The coalition becomes powerful because it offers every faction something it needs while ensuring no faction receives everything it wants. Cassian holds no overriding faction allegiance; his identity is that of a coalition-builder.

#### Ideological Conflict

Cassian remains personally attached to the player even as his methods diverge from theirs. The conflict between them is not based on hatred. Both characters want to prevent further war; they disagree about whether lasting peace can be built on complete truth. The player increasingly believes people must be allowed to confront what happened to the Caelian Empire and why the modern factions were divided. Cassian increasingly believes uncontrolled disclosure will produce civil collapse, factional purges, infrastructure failure, and another generation of war.

Cassian's core belief: peace is not built on truth. It is built on control, compromise, and a story people are willing to accept.

#### Finale Role

Cassian enters the Caelis finale with political legitimacy, legal documents, faction representatives, and a negotiated transition plan. The player reaches Caelis through battles and discovery; Cassian reaches Caelis through agreements, controlled information, and institutional support.

Cassian is not defeated as a conventional final villain. The central confrontation is whether the player accepts Cassian's settlement, exposes what Cassian intends to seal, or destroys the system both sides are attempting to control.

### Campaign Structure

The campaign consists of three acts, reorganized from the existing mission list without increasing the total mission count. Act I introduces the world and the player, Act II develops the political conflict surrounding the player and the recovered Caelian technology, and Act III resolves the mystery of Caelis and the Source.

Every mission should advance at least one of: the player's personal journey, the political conflict, or the mystery surrounding the Caelian Empire. There are no separate self-contained faction campaigns; all factions participate naturally in the same overarching conflict.

#### Act I — Reclamation (~8 missions)

Purpose: introduce gameplay, recovered machine units, Cassian, and the reclamation expedition. Teaches basic synchronization, movement, combat, objectives, deployment, recovery, and machine activation.

The opening begins as a routine joint reclamation expedition investigating a dormant Caelian relay. Recovered machine units provide the tutorial roster. The player is not yet a Conductor.

During the final missions of the act, the relay activates. The activation was intentionally planned by unknown parties; most expedition members believe it was an accident. The player survives and becomes the first publicly known multi-compatible Conductor. Cassian survives because the player saves him. The survivors escape with a small force of reclaimed units.

The act ends with multiple factions attempting to claim both the player and the recovered technology.

Primary questions introduced: Why did the relay activate? Who planned the experiment? Why can the player synchronize with multiple systems?

#### Act II — The Crisis (~20 missions)

This act replaces the separate Coal, Steam, Solar, Wind, and Fusion campaign concepts with one interconnected political crisis involving all factions. The initial conflict begins around ownership of reclaimed Caelian infrastructure and expands naturally rather than feeling like isolated faction stories:

- Coal discovers additional Caelian infrastructure beneath active industrial territory.
- Steam claims legal authority over the relay network.
- Solar offers technical expertise while attempting to gain control of recovered research.
- Wind becomes involved through transport routes, smugglers, refugees, and decentralized settlements.
- Fusion joins later, after becoming capable of partially activating additional Caelian systems.

Major narrative goals:

- The player earns recognition as a Conductor.
- Cassian gains political influence and increasingly solves problems politically that the player cannot solve militarily.
- Recovered Caelian technology becomes increasingly important.
- Several Conductors become allies; several become enemies.
- At least one major Conductor dies through politics rather than battlefield defeat.

Major revelations introduced gradually: modern technologies share Caelian origins; recovered archives contradict official history; Conductor specialization may not be natural.

The act ends after the activation of another Caelian system reveals evidence that Caelis still exists.

Primary questions: Who rewrote history? Why was Caelis hidden? Who benefits from maintaining the lie?

#### Act III — Caelis (~12 missions)

This act concludes the campaign. Caelis is not one large dungeon; it is divided into several meaningful locations. Recommended sequence:

1. Approach the exclusion zone.
2. Temporary alliance between factions.
3. Outer City.
4. Imperial Archive.
5. Conductor Vault.
6. Civic Core.
7. The Source.
8. Cassian's coalition arrives.
9. Final confrontation.

The player gradually learns: the Empire deliberately fragmented; official Caelian history is incomplete; the Source is an integrated energy system; previous multi-compatible Conductors existed. Cassian reveals that he has already learned much of this information and intentionally concealed parts of it while building a political settlement.

The final confrontation is ideological rather than personal. The player and Cassian both want peace; they disagree on whether civilization can survive complete truth. Accord is the primary canonical ending; other endings remain future design goals (see Ending Philosophy).

### Mission Design Principles

Every mission should satisfy at least one of:

- Teach a gameplay mechanic.
- Advance a major character.
- Reveal political information.
- Reveal historical information.
- Change relationships between factions.
- Change Cassian's political influence.
- Introduce or resolve a major mystery.

Avoid filler missions and "go here because another faction exists" missions. Every mission should have lasting consequences that affect later missions. The player should often win battles while losing politically.

### The Resonance War

Nobody should call the conflict the Resonance War during the main story. Characters refer only to the immediate crises they understand: the relay disaster, the struggle over reclaimed Caelian infrastructure, the regional flashpoints of the Act II crisis, and the race to Caelis.

The player spends much of the game believing they are responding to a sequence of separate conflicts. Gradually, they discover shared causes, overlapping conspiracies, and connections to Caelian history. Even then, the characters may not understand that later generations will treat these events as one historical war.

Only in the epilogue should the audience see that historians grouped the scattered rebellions, coups, emergencies, assassinations, proxy conflicts, and territorial struggles under a single name. Suggested final framing:

> "No one who fought believed they were living through history.
> Only later would these scattered conflicts be remembered by a single name.
> The Resonance War."

The meaning of the game's title becomes fully clear only at the end. The Accord ending's final narration closes with historians referring to all previous regional conflicts as "the Resonance War."

### Unreliable History

History should be treated as contested and politically useful. Every faction should preserve a different account of past events.

- Coal textbooks may contradict Solar archives.
- Steam engineering records may dismiss Wind oral histories.
- Fusion scholars may alter or classify evidence.
- Religious institutions may preserve accurate events but interpret them incorrectly.
- Official histories may credit victories to the wrong people.
- Allies may be remembered as traitors.
- Manipulators may be remembered as peacemakers.

The player may know that an official account is false because they personally witnessed what happened. The epilogue may quote a later historical text that contains a mixture of truth, propaganda, omission, and simplification.

Historians may disagree about when the Resonance War began. Some may trace it to the Coal Crisis. Others to the Solar Schism. Others may argue that it began generations earlier with the deliberate fragmentation of the Caelian Empire.

### Caelis

Caelis should function as a central mystery for much of the game. Everyone knows the name. Few or no living people have seen the city.

Maps may end before reaching it. Expeditions disappear. Routes are closed for contradictory reasons. Different factions publicly deny controlling the surrounding territory while quietly preventing access. The factions may share an unspoken agreement that nobody should reach the old capital.

Caelis should not be treated as a simple ruined dungeon. It should be a place whose continued existence threatens the legitimacy of every modern government.

The player should not reach Caelis until late in the story. What they find there should recontextualize the factions, Conductors, the Source, and the supposed fall of the Empire.

### Possible Major Revelations

> **Future design decisions:** these are narrative options, not all mandatory.

- The Caelian Empire intentionally dissolved itself.
- Modern faction specialization was politically imposed rather than scientifically inevitable.
- The player is not the first multi-compatible Conductor. Previous examples were erased from history.
- The original Conductors were capable of coordinating the complete Caelian system.
- The Source does not create infinite energy from nothing but balances and distributes energy across a vast network.
- The Source may be connected to a preserved intelligence, archived Conductor, or distributed machine consciousness.
- Modern governments know more about Caelis than they publicly admit.
- The political rival may discover part of the truth before the player and use it to build a new order.
- A legendary First Conductor may still exist, imprisoned within the Source or kept alive as part of its infrastructure.

### Ending Philosophy

Do not design a single perfect ending. The final choice should involve tradeoffs between truth, stability, freedom, equality, control, and survival. No outcome should be obviously correct, and the conclusion should leave room for players to debate whether the player improved the world, destabilized it, preserved a corrupt peace, or merely replaced one ruling structure with another.

Ending B (Accord) is confirmed canon and the baseline against which all alternative endings are compared. The other endings remain under development.

#### Ending B: Accord (Confirmed Canon)

In the Accord ending, the player supports Cassian's settlement. The player concludes that immediately releasing the complete truth about Caelis, the Source, the imposed faction divisions, and the historical cover-up would destabilize civilization beyond its ability to recover.

**Partial disclosure.** The public learns that:

- The accepted history of the Caelian Empire was incomplete.
- The modern factions share Caelian technological origins.
- The Source is connected to infrastructure across multiple territories.
- No single faction has a legitimate exclusive claim to Caelis.
- The recent regional conflicts were linked more closely than governments admitted.
- A new political and technical structure is required to prevent another war.

**Sealed evidence.** The most destabilizing evidence is sealed — not destroyed — and placed under restricted authority with the stated intention of controlled future release. Whether that release ever occurs should remain uncertain. Sealed material may include:

- Proof that modern Conductor specialization was deliberately imposed.
- Full records of previous multi-compatible Conductors.
- The names of institutions that preserved the original cover-up.
- Evidence that current governments knowingly maintained parts of the exclusion around Caelis.
- Technical information that would allow factions to reproduce generalized Conductors.
- Complete access protocols for the Source.
- Records proving that celebrated historical leaders participated in coercion, assassination, or deliberate fragmentation.
- Information that could immediately invalidate governments, religions, property claims, or faction identities.

**The new authority.** The Source is placed under a new central authority with faction representation (its formal name will be created during later political world-building). Its structure may include delegates from every major faction, representatives from neutral regions, Conductors from multiple disciplines, civil infrastructure officials, technical custodians, limited representation for autonomous machines, independent auditors or archivists, and Cassian as the central executive, mediator, chancellor, or architect of the settlement. No faction gains direct control of the Source, but the authority remains centralized and depends heavily on Cassian's coalition.

**Immediate results:**

- The war ends; major faction armies withdraw or accept ceasefire boundaries.
- Energy distribution stabilizes; civil infrastructure remains operational.
- Caelis is placed under international or coalition administration.
- The Source resumes limited, regulated operation.
- Cassian becomes the recognized architect of peace.
- The old power structure survives in altered form; many existing officials retain influence by joining the new system.
- Some crimes are quietly pardoned to preserve the agreement; some truths remain classified; some faction inequalities continue.
- The player's role is minimized.

**The player's place in history.** Official histories emphasize Cassian's diplomacy, coalition-building, and negotiated settlement. The player may be described as a military agent of the coalition, a field commander who secured Caelis, a dangerous Conductor brought under lawful authority, an anonymous expedition survivor, a disputed figure whose involvement remains classified, a symbolic hero whose actual discoveries are omitted, or a destabilizing actor whose restraint made peace possible.

The player is not necessarily imprisoned or openly betrayed. Cassian may offer them an honored position within the new order: First Conductor of the Caelis Authority, Guardian of the Source, leader of a multinational machine force, technical liaison between factions, or Custodian of the sealed archives. Accepting such a position gives the player influence but binds them to the system they helped create; rejecting it allows the player to leave but further removes them from the official historical record.

**Why the Accord is compelling.** Cassian sincerely believes it is the least destructive available outcome. It prevents immediate collapse; keeps hospitals, transport systems, settlements, and industrial infrastructure operating; prevents any one faction from seizing the Source; and stops the active war. It also protects compromised institutions, limits public knowledge, and places enormous power in a new centralized authority. The Accord is the strongest short-term outcome for stability; its long-term cost is that a political system built on incomplete truth may reproduce many of the conditions that caused the Resonance War.

**Epilogue framing.** A possible passage:

> "Through negotiation, restraint, and the historic Caelis Accord, Cassian brought an end to the crises that had divided the continent.
> The surviving factions surrendered their claims to the Source and entered a new era of shared administration.
> Later histories would remember Cassian as the architect of peace."

The player knows this account is incomplete. They know which evidence was sealed, which compromises were made, and who died so that the agreement could survive. They also know that without Cassian's settlement, the war may have continued. The emotional result should be a victory that feels stable, compromised, and personally diminishing: Cassian wins history. The player helps save civilization but does not control the story that civilization tells about itself.

#### Alternative Endings (Under Development)

> **Future design decisions:** these directions remain provisional and are measured against Accord as the baseline.

- The truth is exposed, causing immediate collapse but opening the possibility of long-term reform.
- The Source is divided or distributed among regions.
- The Source is destroyed, restoring scarcity and weakening centralized power.
- Humans, Conductors, and autonomous machines form a negotiated system of shared control.
- Cassian creates a stable but authoritarian order without the player's support.
- One faction gains dominance while claiming to have saved civilization.

### Narrative Rules

Follow these rules when expanding the game's story, quests, and characters:

- Do not create purely evil factions.
- Do not make the player automatically correct.
- Do not solve every conflict through unity.
- Every faction should betray itself at least once.
- Every faction should contain sympathetic and corrupt characters.
- Important allies may die outside combat.
- Major characters may be removed through political maneuvering.
- Winning battles should sometimes make the world worse.
- The player should frequently act with incomplete information.
- Every major revelation should recontextualize earlier events.
- Historical records should contradict one another.
- The Source should remain mysterious until late in the story.
- Do not reveal early that the modern energy systems are fragments of the original Caelian architecture.
- Conductors should be politically and economically important, not just powerful combat units.
- The player's mixed-faction capabilities should create fear and political instability, not universal admiration.
- The player's rival should remain morally understandable.
- The ending should not provide complete certainty about whether the final outcome was right.

### Narrative Design Goal

Create a tactical RPG whose story is remembered for political intrigue, tragic consequences, factional complexity, unreliable history, and major twists.

Players should finish the game still arguing about:

- What really happened to the Caelian Empire.
- Why Caelis was hidden.
- Whether the Source should ever have been restored.
- Which faction was most justified.
- Whether the player's rival saved civilization or stole the future.
- Whether exposing the truth was worth the cost.
- Whether the player changed history or simply became another person history chose to forget.

### Terminology and Continuity Decisions

The contradictions previously flagged between this canon and existing prototype material are resolved as follows:

- **Conductor is the single term.** The leader each side protects — and its once-per-battle power — is a Conductor, in-fiction and throughout this document (replacing the earlier "Commander"/"Captain" wording). Code and script internals still use the legacy `Commander`/`Captain` naming; renaming internals is a mechanical follow-up with no design impact.
- **Classes exist in every faction.** All six gameplay classes (Scout, Fighter, Defender, Gunner, Mage, Priest) exist within each of the five energy factions; a faction simply fields some classes more prevalently than others. The player's canon multi-discipline synchronization is expressed through mixed-faction squad building.
- **Story structure is implemented.** The campaign is now 77 missions in three acts matching the structure above: Act 1 "Reclamation" (missions 1–22), Act 2 "The Crisis" (missions 23–62), and Act 3 "Caelis" (missions 63–77, original content). Every mission carries authored briefing/debriefing text (`MISSION_STORIES` in `scripts/story_quest_catalog.gd`) written to `documentation/Resonance_War_Campaign_Narrative.md`, which supersedes the old two-act framing. The older reference-mission titles are retained as cast and beat anchors.
- **Sky premise de-emphasized.** The earlier sky-island backdrop is legacy atmosphere art only, not setting canon; see the Presentation section.

## The Player Experience

A match should be easy to understand, tactically interesting, and last about five to eight minutes. The player makes a small number of important decisions:

- Which unit to deploy
- Which lane to place it in
- Whether to attack, defend, or prepare for a later turn
- How to combine unit classes and abilities
- When to use the Conductor's once-per-battle power

After deployment, units act automatically. The strategy comes from anticipating how the board will resolve.

## How a Match Is Played

### Objective

Each side has a Conductor at opposite ends of the battlefield. Reduce the enemy Conductor's health to zero before the enemy defeats yours.

### Battlefield

The battlefield contains three lanes and seven columns. The player controls the left side and the opponent controls the right side.

```text
Player                                                     Enemy
Conductor  [ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]  Conductor
Conductor  [ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]  Conductor
Conductor  [ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]  Conductor
```

Units enter from their owner's edge and advance toward the opposing Conductor. Units normally remain in their chosen lane unless an ability moves them.

### Squad

Before battle, each side prepares a squad of up to 8 units:

- One Vanguard is always available at the start of the match.
- Up to 7 other units form the draw pile.
- The player begins with the Vanguard and three drawn units.
- Reserve cards are shuffled at the start of every battle; the Vanguard remains the guaranteed first card.
- A bench can hold up to six units.
- A squad may contain no more than two copies of the same unit.
- The Barracks begins with one copy of every base-set card and permanently keeps every card reward, including duplicates.
- Players can drag cards between Barracks and Squad; clicking a selected squad card removes one copy.
- Players can drag selected cards to reorder the squad or right-click a card to make it the Vanguard.
- PvE opponents follow the same squad, opening hand, hand limit, draw, and deck-exhaustion rules.
- Each campaign encounter defines its own eight-card enemy squad and Conductor skill.

Every unit has a deployment cost, attack, health, movement distance, attack range, class, a race (Human, Ogur, Lambkin, or Felyne), and one or more abilities. Race is a data trait ported from the reference database with no battlefield presence of its own; aura skills such as Inspire Lambkin key off it.

The current prototype roster contains 48 unpromoted units and 40 promoted forms linked into family trees. Promoted forms still enter the reward pool as independent cards, and a level-5 copy can also be converted into its next form in the Kinetic Crucible (see Kinetic Crucible).

Campaign missions only award implemented cards listed for that exact story quest in the reference data. Cards listed solely for events, raids, arenas, or gacha are not inserted into campaign pools.

### Mana

Deploying a unit locks its Mana cost while it remains on the battlefield.

- Both sides begin with a Mana capacity of 2.
- Capacity increases by 2 at the start of each later turn, up to 10.
- Available Mana equals capacity minus Mana locked by active units.
- When a unit is defeated, its locked Mana becomes available again.

This makes committing an expensive unit a continuing resource decision rather than a one-turn payment.

### Turn Sequence

Each turn has four phases.

1. **Start**
   - Increase Mana capacity by 2, up to 10.
   - Recalculate available Mana after active-unit locks.
   - Draw one unit.
   - Resolve start-of-turn effects.

2. **Command**
   - Deploy one or more units the player can afford.
   - Units are placed in an open tile on the player's deployment edge.
   - Reposition deployed units to a reachable tile in any other lane at the same column.
   - Use the Conductor power if it is available.
   - End the phase when ready.

3. **Resolution**
   - Units act one at a time, beginning with the unit closest to the enemy Conductor.
   - A unit first moves toward the enemy by up to its movement distance.
   - Movement stops early when another unit still occupies the next cell.
   - After movement finishes, the unit attacks if a valid target is in range.
   - Abilities, healing, status effects, and defeated units are resolved immediately.

4. **End**
   - Check whether either Conductor has been defeated.
   - Resolve end-of-turn effects.
   - The opposing side begins its turn.

The match continues until a Conductor reaches zero health. If both Conductors are defeated during the same action, the acting player wins.

## Combat Rules

- A unit cannot move through another unit.
- Frontmost units resolve first, so vacated cells are available to units moving later in the same phase.
- Opposing units in the same lane block one another.
- A unit attacks the nearest valid target unless an ability says otherwise.
- Damage reduces health immediately.
- A unit is removed when its health reaches zero.
- If no unit blocks an attack at the end of a lane, the enemy Conductor takes the damage.
- The first and seventh columns are deployment and Conductor zones. Units may deploy there, but opposing units stop in the adjacent column and attack into the zone instead of entering it.
- Newly deployed units move and attack during the resolution phase of the turn in which they are placed.
- Deployed units may reposition any number of times during the Command phase without losing their normal activation. They may cross friendly units, but cannot cross enemies or finish on an occupied cell.
- A lane change may cross multiple rows, but every crossed tile must be unoccupied.
- A unit hit by a Defender's Taunting Strike cannot change lanes for its next two turns.
- Effects with the same timing resolve in board order, from the enemy side toward the acting side, then from the top lane downward.

Secondary unit skills use five timing keywords:

- **Warcry:** resolves once immediately when deployed, before movement or attack.
- **Chant:** resolves at the start of its controller's turn.
- **Strike:** resolves whenever the unit attacks, after its normal class attack.
- **Reaction:** resolves after an enemy's Strike when the unit is attacked.
- **Aura:** remains active continuously while its source is on the battlefield.

The ability sequence is: Deploy → Warcry → start-of-turn Chants → movement → attack → Strike → enemy Reaction. Auras remain active throughout.

Board order and targeting are deterministic except where an ability explicitly specifies a chance or random target.

## Unit Classes

| Class | Movement | Range | Battlefield role |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Scout | 3 | 1 | **Double Strike:** attacks twice each attack action |
| Fighter | 2 | 1 | **Fury:** permanently gains +1 ATK after attacking a unit or Conductor |
| Defender | 2 | 1 | **Taunting Strike:** prevents its target from changing lanes for two turns |
| Gunner | 1 | 3 | **Piercing Shot:** damages every enemy in range along its lane |
| Mage | 1 | 3 | **Blast:** adjacent enemies take half the Mage's ATK as splash damage |
| Priest | 1 | 2 | **Heal:** gives 2 HP to the lowest-health other ally before moving |

Classes define basic behavior, while individual abilities give each unit a distinct purpose.

### Kinetic Crucible

Unit progression belongs to each individual copy and persists between sessions.
Duplicate units appear separately in the collection and squad workshop, so every
copy can have its own level and Crucible progress. Units begin at level 1 and
advance to level 5 by consuming spare collection copies in the Kinetic Crucible.
Level thresholds are 3, 6, 12, and 24 points. An unrelated donor
grants 1 point, a donor of the same class grants 2 points, and another copy of the
same unit grants 5 points. Overflow carries into the next level. Each level
raises the copy's ATK and max HP by 10% of its base value (level 5 ≈ 1.4×),
and its secondary skill uses the matching rank of the reference value table:
level 1 is the base effect and level 5 is the strongest rank.

Level-5 units with an implemented next form are promotion-ready. Promoting
converts the copy 1:1 into its promoted form at no additional cost: the copy
keeps its identity (saved squads now field the promoted unit), starts over at
level 1 so it can be levelled again, and a fresh level-1 copy of the base unit
is restored to the collection on the next sync. Promoted forms with their own
next form can be levelled and promoted again along the same family tree.

The Crucible uses the same two-panel collection language as Formation Command.
Every individual owned copy appears in Reserves on the left. The first selected
copy becomes the enhancement target at the top of the right panel; any number of
additional copies can then be queued beneath it and merged together. Target and
queue cards can be clicked to clear or remove them before committing.

The Crucible includes an “extras only” donor filter. When enabled, unsafe donor
copies remain visible but disabled, and only unit types with at least two active
copies can be queued. Consumed copies disappear from the collection and are
removed from any saved squad that referenced them.

For development, the Crucible also exposes a clearly labeled debug inventory
button that grants enough copies to leave at least four active copies of every
implemented unit without resetting existing levels or merge progress.

## Example Turn

The player begins a turn with 4 available Mana. An enemy Defender blocks the center lane, while the top lane is open.

1. The player deploys a 2-Mana Scout in the top lane.
2. The player deploys a 2-Mana Priest behind a damaged Fighter in the center lane.
3. During resolution, the Fighter attacks the Defender.
4. The Priest heals the Fighter.
5. The newly deployed Scout advances immediately and attacks if it reaches a target.
6. The newly deployed Priest heals, advances, and attacks if a target is in range.

The player has traded immediate power for pressure in one lane and durability in another.

## Conductor Powers

Each Conductor equips one power for battle: Rally, Bloodlust, Aid, Healing Wave, Shield, Last Stand, Lightning Burst, or Firestorm. Friendly-unit effects normally last one turn unless a skill explicitly states otherwise.

Conductor selection changes a squad's strategy without adding another unit to the board.

Campaign victories award one random card from that quest's eligible drop list. Drop weight halves with each additional Star Rarity, making lower-star cards progressively more likely. Earned card unlocks persist and expand strategic options rather than directly increasing unit statistics.

## Presentation

Battlefield and menu backdrops may retain sky and floating-island atmosphere from earlier art direction, but this is aesthetic only and not setting canon. The post-Caelian world of the Narrative Foundation is authoritative, and the sky aspect should not drive story, faction, or quest design.

The visual direction combines:

- Illustrated character cards
- Readable, small battlefield figures
- Bright elemental effects
- A clean interface designed for mouse and touch
- Strong lane, range, target, and movement indicators

Combat information must remain readable even when several abilities resolve in sequence.

The current prototype uses its official unit illustrations as the visual source of
truth. Its interface should feel like a richly illustrated chibi fantasy strategy
game presented through an ornate, tactile storybook:

- Deep ink navy and charcoal establish field and panel surfaces.
- Warm parchment, antique brass, and muted cream define hierarchy and readable text.
- Class colors communicate roles on cards and the battlefield without replacing the
  shared brass-and-parchment interface language.
- Compact bevels, double-line borders, and subtle shadows take precedence over
  luminous science-fiction glass.
- Background paintings remain low-contrast atmosphere so units carry the strongest
  color, contrast, and detail.
- Portrait sprites appear in menus, rewards, decks, and workshop cards. Full-body
  sprites remain exclusive to the battlefield.

## Technical Direction

The project will use Godot 4 and target desktop first at a touch-friendly 16:9 resolution. Unit and ability data will be stored as Godot Resources so content can be balanced without changing combat code.

Battle activation planning is separated from animation and interface code in `BattleSimulator`. It owns seeded randomness, activation order, projected movement, target selection, skill timing metadata, structured event records, and deterministic replay serialization. The presentation layer consumes those decisions for animation and audio, while a headless balance harness audits every configured campaign encounter.

The current presentation layer previews each side's upcoming activation order before resolution, identifies unit class and secondary-skill timing in the combat log, animates combat results, and displays persistent counters for Taunt, Immobilise, Fury, buffs, and debuffs.

Combat presentation uses eased movement and class-specific actions: melee lunges, ranged projectiles, healing pulses, hit recoil, sequential Double Strikes, multi-target Blast and Piercing feedback, and a defeat dissolve before defeated units leave the board.

Battle audio is synthesized locally and synchronized with deployment, movement, class attacks, healing, status application, shields, impacts, and defeat. Players can select full volume, half volume, or mute; Conductor impacts also apply a brief screen shake.

Presentation settings persist across sessions. A settings gear in the upper-right interface contains the shared battle/replay speed, sound, animation-skip, reduced-motion, and combat-log controls. Reduced motion shortens movement and suppresses screen shake. Live battle resolution is intentionally not pausable.

The main menu exposes the ten most recently completed battles through an event-driven replay viewer. It reconstructs deployments and board movement, animates attacks and defeats, supports play/pause and single-step transport, uses the shared speed setting, displays the original seed and event position, pages between older and newer replays, compares the recorded player and enemy squads, and reports final-state desynchronization. Replay history is newest-first and automatically removes the oldest entry after the tenth. Core unit damage, healing, shield absorption, and Conductor damage calculations live in `BattleSimulator`.
